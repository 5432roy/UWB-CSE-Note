## Matrix and vector multiplication

Multiplying an $a \times b$ matrix by a $b \times c$ matrix produces an $a \times c$ matrix.

Multiplying a matrix by a vector produces a vector. There are two ways to view this operation:

- **Row view:** Each value in the result is the dot product of a row of the matrix with the input vector.
- **Column view:** Each scalar entry of the input vector multiplies the corresponding column of the matrix. Summing these scaled columns gives the result vector.

### Matrix–vector example

Use the same matrix and vector to compare the two methods:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\underline{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The dimensions are $(2 \times 3)(3 \times 1)$, so $A\underline{v}$ is a $2 \times 1$ vector.

With the row view, take each row's dot product with $\underline{v}$:

$$
A\underline{v} = \begin{bmatrix}
2(1) + (-1)(2) + 3(-1) \\
0(1) + 4(2) + 1(-1)
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

With the column view, scale each column by the corresponding entry of $\underline{v}$ and add:

$$
A\underline{v} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
+ 2\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
- 1\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Matrix–matrix example

Using the same $A$, let

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The dimensions are $(2 \times 3)(3 \times 2)$, so $AB$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix. Each entry is the dot product of a row of $A$ with a column of $B$:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix}
2(1) + (-1)(2) + 3(-1) & 2(2) + (-1)(0) + 3(3) \\
0(1) + 4(2) + 1(-1) & 0(2) + 4(0) + 1(3)
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 13 \\ 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each column of $AB$ is $A$ multiplied by the corresponding column of $B$.

## Vector spaces

A **vector space** is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication:

- If $\underline{u},\underline{v} \in V$, then $\underline{u}+\underline{v} \in V$.
- If $\underline{v} \in V$ and $c$ is a scalar from the underlying field, then $c\underline{v} \in V$. For $\mathbb{R}^D$, the scalars are real numbers.

These rules mean that adding vectors from the same vector space or multiplying a vector by a scalar keeps the result in that space. Together, they imply that any finite linear combination $c_1\underline{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\underline{v}_k$ also belongs to $V$ when all $\underline{v}_i \in V$.

A vector space satisfies the following rules for $\underline{u},\underline{v},\underline{w} \in V$ and scalars $a,b$ from the underlying field:

| Rule | Equation |
|---|---|
| Closure under vector addition | $\underline{u}+\underline{v} \in V$ |
| Closure under scalar multiplication | $a\underline{v} \in V$ |
| Addition is commutative | $\underline{u}+\underline{v}=\underline{v}+\underline{u}$ |
| Addition is associative | $(\underline{u}+\underline{v})+\underline{w}=\underline{u}+(\underline{v}+\underline{w})$ |
| A zero vector exists | $\underline{v}+\underline{0}=\underline{v}$ |
| Every vector has an additive inverse | $\underline{v}+(-\underline{v})=\underline{0}$ |
| Scalar multiplication is associative | $a(b\underline{v})=(ab)\underline{v}$ |
| Multiplication by 1 preserves the vector | $1\underline{v}=\underline{v}$ |
| Scalars distribute over vector addition | $a(\underline{u}+\underline{v})=a\underline{u}+a\underline{v}$ |
| Scalar addition distributes over a vector | $(a+b)\underline{v}=a\underline{v}+b\underline{v}$ |

### Basis, linear independence, and span

The **span** of a set of vectors is the set of all their linear combinations. The vectors span a space $V$ if every vector in $V$ can be written as a linear combination of them.

Vectors $\underline{v}_1,\ldots,\underline{v}_k$ are **linearly independent** if

$$
c_1\underline{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\underline{v}_k=\underline{0}
$$

holds only when $c_1=\cdots=c_k=0$.

A **basis** of $V$ is a set of vectors that is both **linearly independent and spans $V$**. Each vector in that set is a basis vector. Every vector in $V$ has a unique representation as a linear combination of the basis vectors.

For example, the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is

$$
\underline{e}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\underline{e}_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

They span $\mathbb{R}^2$ because

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}=a\underline{e}_1+b\underline{e}_2
$$

for any real $a,b$. They are linearly independent because $a\underline{e}_1+b\underline{e}_2=\underline{0}$ forces $a=b=0$. Each is also a unit vector, with length 1, but either one alone does not span $\mathbb{R}^2$.

## Dot product and angle

*Tentative completion of the missing detail: the relationship between the dot product and the angle between two vectors.*

For real vectors $\underline{u}$ and $\underline{v}$, the **dot product** satisfies

$$
\underline{u} \cdot \underline{v} = \sum_i u_i v_i = \|\underline{u}\|\|\underline{v}\|\cos\theta,
$$

where $\|\underline{u}\|$ and $\|\underline{v}\|$ are their lengths and $\theta$ is the angle between them. For nonzero vectors,

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\underline{u}\cdot \underline{v}}{\|\underline{u}\|\|\underline{v}\|}.
$$

Without a specified subscript, $\|\underline{x}\|$ normally denotes the **Euclidean norm**, where $p=2$. This is the Euclidean distance from $\underline{x}$ to the origin.

## Inner product spaces

An **inner product** is an operation that takes two vectors and returns a scalar. The usual dot product is one example:

$$
\langle \underline{u},\underline{v}\rangle
= \underline{u}\cdot\underline{v}
= \sum_i u_i v_i.
$$

An **inner product space** is a vector space equipped with an inner product. For example, $\mathbb{R}^D$ with the usual dot product is an inner product space.

The dot product names the operation; an inner product space is the space together with that operation.

### Vector, normed, and inner product spaces

A **normed vector space** is a vector space equipped with a norm, which measures vector length. Each type of space adds structure:

| Space | What it provides | What it allows |
|---|---|---|
| Vector space | Vector addition and scalar multiplication | Sums and linear combinations |
| Normed vector space | A vector space plus a norm $\|\underline{x}\|$ | Lengths and distances |
| Inner product space | A vector space plus an inner product $\langle\underline{x},\underline{y}\rangle$ | Angles and perpendicularity, as well as lengths and distances |

For example, take vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$
\underline{x}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\underline{y}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The vector-space structure allows addition:

$$
\underline{x}+\underline{y}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Equipping the space with the Euclidean norm gives a length:

$$
\|\underline{x}\|_2=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5.
$$

Equipping the space with the usual dot product measures alignment:

$$
\langle\underline{x},\underline{y}\rangle=3(1)+4(0)=3.
$$

Every inner product defines a norm through

$$
\|\underline{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\underline{x},\underline{x}\rangle}.
$$

**Every inner product space is also a normed space, and every normed space is also a vector space.** The reverse does not always hold for the chosen norm. For example, the norm $\|\underline{x}\|_1=\sum_i |x_i|$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not induced by an inner product.

## Transpose

The **transpose** of a matrix swaps its rows and columns. It is written as $A^T$, with $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$. If $A$ is $a \times b$, then $A^T$ is $b \times a$.

For the matrix used above:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The following relations hold when the matrix dimensions permit the operations, with $k$ a scalar:

- Transposing twice gives the original matrix: $(A^T)^T = A$.
- Transpose distributes over addition: $(A+B)^T = A^T+B^T$.
- A scalar factor stays the same: $(kA)^T = kA^T$.
- **Transposing a product reverses the order:** $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$.
- A **symmetric matrix** satisfies $A^T = A$.

Using the multiplication example above:

$$
(AB)^T = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 7 \\ 13 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = B^T A^T.
$$

## Trace

The **trace** of $A$ is the sum of its diagonal entries. **Trace is defined only for square matrices**, which have the same number of rows and columns.

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_i A_{ii}.
$$

For example:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\operatorname{tr}(C) = 2 + 3 = 5.
$$

### Trace properties

- Addition: $\operatorname{tr}(A+B) = \operatorname{tr}(A)+\operatorname{tr}(B)$ for square matrices of the same size.
- Scalar multiplication: $\operatorname{tr}(kA) = k\operatorname{tr}(A)$ for a scalar $k$ and square matrix $A$.
- Transpose: $\operatorname{tr}(A^T) = \operatorname{tr}(A)$ for square $A$, because transposing preserves the diagonal entries.
- **Cyclic property:** $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times m$, both products are square, so this holds even if $A$ and $B$ are rectangular.
- For three factors, $\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(BCA) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB)$ whenever the products are defined and square. This allows cyclic rotations of the factors, not arbitrary reorderings.
- Identity: $\operatorname{tr}(I_n) = n$.

Using the earlier matrices $A$ and $B$:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 13 \\ 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
BA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 & 5 \\ 4 & -2 & 6 \\ -2 & 13 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Although the products have different sizes, their traces agree:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB) = -3 + 3 = 0,
\qquad
\operatorname{tr}(BA) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0.
$$

### Proof of the cyclic property

Let $A$ be $m \times n$ and $B$ be $n \times m$. Each diagonal entry of $AB$ is

$$
(AB)_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}B_{ji}.
$$

Summing these entries gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(AB)
&= \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}B_{ji} \\
&= \sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m} B_{ji}A_{ij} \\
&= \sum_{j=1}^{n}(BA)_{jj} \\
&= \operatorname{tr}(BA).
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle step exchanges the order of finite sums and uses the fact that **individual entries are scalars**, so $A_{ij}B_{ji} = B_{ji}A_{ij}$.

For three matrices whose products are defined and square, group two factors together and apply the same result:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(ABC)
= \operatorname{tr}\bigl(A(BC)\bigr)
= \operatorname{tr}\bigl((BC)A\bigr)
= \operatorname{tr}(BCA).
$$

Repeating this step gives $\operatorname{tr}(CAB)$, proving the cyclic property for three factors.

## Matrix rank

The **rank** of a matrix $A$, written $\operatorname{rank}(A)$, is the dimension of the space spanned by its columns. Equivalently, it is the maximum number of linearly independent columns. This also equals the maximum number of linearly independent rows: **row rank and column rank are equal**.

For an $m \times n$ matrix,

$$
0 \leq \operatorname{rank}(A) \leq \min(m,n).
$$

### Calculating rank by row reduction

1. Use elementary row operations to reduce the matrix to row echelon form: swap rows, multiply a row by a nonzero scalar, or add a multiple of one row to another.
2. Count the pivots, which are the leading nonzero entries of the nonzero rows in echelon form.
3. The number of pivots, equivalently the number of nonzero rows in echelon form, is the rank. Row operations preserve rank.

For example:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
2 & 4 & 6 \\
0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Apply $R_2 \leftarrow R_2-2R_1$, then swap $R_2$ and $R_3$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
2 & 4 & 6 \\
0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
0 & 1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The echelon form has two pivots, so $\operatorname{rank}(M)=2$. In the original matrix, the second row is twice the first, while the first and third rows are linearly independent.

### Full rank

An $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has **full rank** if its rank is as large as its dimensions allow:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\min(m,n).
$$

- **Full column rank:** $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$, so all columns are linearly independent. This requires $m \geq n$.
- **Full row rank:** $\operatorname{rank}(A)=m$, so all rows are linearly independent. This requires $n \geq m$.
- For an $n \times n$ square matrix, full rank means $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$. Both its rows and its columns are linearly independent, and the matrix is invertible.

A matrix is **rank deficient** if $\operatorname{rank}(A)<\min(m,n)$. The $3 \times 3$ matrix $M$ above has rank 2, so it is rank deficient.

## Determinant

The **determinant**, written $\det(A)$, is a scalar defined for a square matrix. Geometrically, it gives the signed volume-scaling factor of the linear transformation represented by $A$; in two dimensions, this is a signed area-scaling factor.

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix,

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det(A)=ad-bc.
$$

For example, using the earlier matrix $C$:

$$
\det(C)=\det\begin{bmatrix}2&5\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}
=2(3)-5(-1)=11.
$$

For larger square matrices, the determinant can be calculated by **cofactor expansion** along any row or column. Expanding along row $i$ gives

$$
\det(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}A_{ij}\det(A_{\widehat{i},\widehat{j}}),
$$

where $A_{\widehat{i},\widehat{j}}$ is the matrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. The determinant of a $1 \times 1$ matrix $[a]$ is $a$.

### Determinant properties

For $n \times n$ matrices $A,B$ and a scalar $k$:

- **Invertibility and rank:** $\det(A)\neq0$ if and only if $A$ is invertible, equivalently $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$. A rank-deficient square matrix has determinant 0.
- Product: $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$.
- Transpose: $\det(A^T)=\det(A)$.
- Identity: $\det(I_n)=1$.
- Inverse: $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det(A)$ when $A$ is invertible.
- Scalar multiplication: $\det(kA)=k^n\det(A)$.
- For a diagonal or triangular matrix, the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries.
- Swapping two rows changes the determinant's sign.
- Multiplying one row by $k$ multiplies the determinant by $k$.
- Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged.
- The same row-operation rules also hold for columns.

In general, $\det(A+B)\neq\det(A)+\det(B)$; the determinant does not distribute over matrix addition.

### Geometric meaning and example

For a real square matrix, **$|\det(A)|$ is the factor by which the transformation scales area in two dimensions or volume in higher dimensions**. A positive determinant preserves orientation; a negative determinant reverses it. A zero determinant collapses the space into fewer dimensions.

For example, let

$$
S=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiplication by $S$ doubles the horizontal coordinate and triples the vertical coordinate:

$$
S\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1\\3x_2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The unit square has corners $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, and $(1,1)$ and area 1. Its image has corners $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,3)$, and $(2,3)$, forming a rectangle with area $2\times3=6$. This matches $\det(S)=6$: every area is multiplied by 6.

## Matrix inverse

The **inverse** of an $n\times n$ square matrix $A$ is a matrix $A^{-1}$ satisfying

$$
AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_n.
$$

It reverses the transformation performed by $A$. **An inverse exists if and only if $\det(A)\neq0$, equivalently if $A$ has full rank $n$.** This equivalence applies to square matrices.

For the scaling matrix $S$ above,

$$
S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac12&0\\0&\frac13\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
SS^{-1}=S^{-1}S=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The inverse halves the horizontal coordinate and divides the vertical coordinate by 3, undoing the scaling. If a square matrix has determinant 0, it collapses dimensions and loses information, so its transformation cannot be reversed by an inverse.
